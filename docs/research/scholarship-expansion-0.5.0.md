## Proposed manuscript paragraphs

### A. The interpreter belongs inside the problem of capture

Composition without capture must constrain the scholar who reconstructs it. An interpretation can give every person several affiliations yet reserve to its author the power to decide what those affiliations ultimately mean. Its generosity then depends on a monopoly over the common description. The question is whether a participant can contest the account of humanity through which inclusion is offered while continuing to share institutions, resources and obligations. A defensible reconstruction must allow disagreement with its poetic vocabulary to count as political argument. Otherwise, passage between states remains available only within a world whose interpretive boundaries someone else has fixed.

Viscomi supplies a methodological restraint on this authority. His account of the workshop makes production part of meaning and resists both excusing execution in the name of vision and decoding every mark as a symbol. Decoration can organize attention while also serving the practical requirements of etching and inking [@viscomi1982Workshop, author reprint web pp. 3–8, compilation PDF pp. 60–65]. The implication here is that neither an attractive political reading nor an exact transcription exhausts an illuminated page. A claim about reciprocity needs an account of dramatic speech and visual relations; a claim about a particular mark needs alternatives that include medium and execution. Interpretation becomes more answerable when it identifies what could defeat it. [Viscomi, complete author reprint, especially web pp. 5–8](https://viscomi.sites.oasis.unc.edu/viscomi/coursepack/criticism_etc/Viscomi-%20Illuminated%20Printing.pdf#page=62).

This restraint does not place political criticism in suspension until technical experts agree. It makes expertise itself limited and challengeable. A printmaker can question an inference from a mark to an intention without deciding whether a narrative legitimates domination. A feminist reading can expose a distribution of agency without pretending that every contour was planned to encode it. The manuscript should consequently state separately what a feature looks like, what process may explain it and what political significance the proposed interpretation assigns it. No participant in that inquiry gains an unlimited authority from having supplied one kind of evidence.

### B. Plate 27: reciprocal exchange inside a claim about origins

Jerusalem 27 offers a stronger difficulty than a choice between national isolation and international fellowship. Its closing stanza imagines reciprocal movement among lands and their mutual construction of Jerusalem. Yet the encompassing address places the inhabitants of earth within the religion of Jesus, derives the addressed ancestors through a Druidic genealogy and concludes by calling Israel to follow Jesus. Read together, the passages allow the possibility that a hospitable common world also prescribes the ultimate meaning of its guests' histories [@blakeJerusalem, plate 27, prose E171 and E174; 27:85–88, E173]. The constitutional question generated here is whether reciprocity includes the power to reject that prescription. Movement in both directions does not settle who authors the destination. [Blake, complete plate 27 transcription](https://blake.lib.asu.edu/html/jerusalem.html#jews).

Kaplan connects this address to the poem's criticism of patriarchal possession: a family elevated over the rest of the world can become an instrument of destruction [@kaplan1996Patriarchy, pp. 68–69]. This gives the national argument an internal rather than merely external test. A nation can participate in exchange while retaining an exclusive right to decide which members embody its authentic history. Conversely, a federation can condemn national self-enclosure while absorbing every people's account of its origins into its own story. The proposed reconstruction must refuse both privileges. A people should be able to dispute a common narrative without thereby surrendering its voice in shared affairs; its officers should face challenges from those whom its own narrative misrecognizes. [Kaplan, pp. 68–69](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.kaplan#p68).

Heppner's reading of the early drawing catalogued as Butlin 92 makes the woman-city an agent rather than simply a destination. He proposes reading a supposed number as Gog's name and understands the descending Jerusalem as driving him away. His comparison with biblical narratives and earlier designs makes compositional transformation consequential: the image can generate a prophetic relation rather than merely reproduce an antecedent text [@heppner1986Jerusalem, pp. 4–5, 9–10]. This reading prevents us from treating every female personification as passive by definition. It also opens a harder question for the political reconstruction: whose agency is represented when a nation appears as a woman? The force of a collective personification cannot stand in for the powers of the women, workers or dissenters associated with it. [Heppner, pp. 9–10, with the drawing reproduced on p. 6](https://bq.blakearchive.org/20.1.heppner#p9).

### C. Anti-patriarchal fraternity and the right to refuse a role

Kaplan's central distinction sharpens the manuscript's gender objection. An attack on possessive patriarchs need not produce equal authority between genders. A brotherhood can redistribute power among men while leaving women responsible for the relations through which that fraternity becomes possible [@kaplan1996Patriarchy, pp. 70–71, 77–78]. The relevant rival interpretation therefore accepts much of the emancipatory reading before challenging its conclusion. Deliverance from jealous ownership, criticism of punitive morality and affirmation of sexual life may coexist with a hierarchy of creative subjects and emanated participants. Listing those emancipatory commitments does not answer the objection. The reconstruction has to explain which relations it can inherit and which it must transform. [Kaplan, pp. 77–78](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.kaplan#p77).

The contemporary test is a right to refuse a function while retaining a relationship. A participant may reject the duty to mediate another person's creativity without rejecting love, collective history or common work. Someone may refuse to speak as a nation's mother, authentic descendant or cultural translator while still claiming authority over its institutions. If refusal is intelligible only as separation from the whole, an apparently reciprocal order has concealed a prescribed division of labor. Remaining must include the power to propose different purposes for the common institution, to organize with others around them and to contest who interprets the inherited vocabulary. These powers require an independent defense; the person/state distinction gives them a question, not a complete warrant.

The plate 99 dispute makes that test unusually precise. Kaplan disagrees with Mellor's interpretation of Jerusalem's musculature, attributing it instead to problems of drawing and the influence of Michelangelo. He nevertheless locates absorption in the embrace and its narrative setting [@kaplan1996Patriarchy, p. 82 n. 27]. Agreement on the political objection thus does not establish agreement on its visual evidence. A stronger response must distinguish the contested bodily feature from the relation inferred between the figures. It cannot dismiss the larger criticism merely by offering another explanation of musculature, or certify that criticism by treating bodily appearance as an unambiguous code. [Kaplan's explicit disagreement, p. 82 n. 27](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.kaplan#p82).

The final five lines also complicate a complete disappearance thesis: their last act names the emanations Jerusalem [@blakeJerusalem, 99:1–5, E258–259]. Naming can register persistence without establishing an equal capacity to initiate, refuse or judge. Conversely, an image of union need not by itself demonstrate the elimination of every difference. The issue is what account of agency makes these features cohere. The political construction should therefore treat reciprocal union and assimilation as rival readings whose explanatory burdens remain visible. Its own commitment is that common life must leave participants able to contest their assigned part. That commitment cannot depend on proving that the poem's conclusion has already achieved it. [Blake, plate 99 transcription](https://blake.lib.asu.edu/html/jerusalem.html#J99.1;E258).

### D. Counterpublics make remaining more than individual permission

Fraser makes the right to remain collective as well as individual. Her counterpublics provide settings in which subordinated people develop interpretations of their needs and identities; their withdrawal and regrouping can support intervention in wider public debate. She also warns that counterpublics can reproduce exclusion [@fraser1992PublicSphere, pp. 122–124]. A federation should therefore protect the formation of opposition within and across recognized nations without equating every opposition with a new sovereign unit. A women's forum, a workers' publication or an unaffiliated residents' assembly may need resources and access to common institutions while refusing the claim to represent an entire people. Cultural plurality cannot be measured only by the organizations already awarded seats. [Fraser, pp. 122–124, scan PDF pp. 9–10](https://www.unm.edu/~sromano/english540/Fraser%2C%20Subaltern%20Counterpublics.pdf#page=9).

Her argument about participation also exceeds the removal of formal barriers: unequal social relations can continue to structure discussion after a forum admits everyone [@fraser1992PublicSphere, pp. 118–121]. In this reconstruction, reimbursement for a hearing cannot be assumed to neutralize dependence on an employer or an association that controls housing. Support must reach the conditions under which people prepare, speak and return to daily life. A study should therefore examine who can lose work, care or accommodation through contestation, alongside who appears on a participant list. [Fraser, pp. 118–121](https://www.unm.edu/~sromano/english540/Fraser%2C%20Subaltern%20Counterpublics.pdf#page=7).

This changes what the translation obligation must secure. A claimant needs more than permission to submit reasons in an authorized language: people need places and time to formulate reasons together before presenting them to a body they oppose. Funding only official national representatives can make dissent dependent on the very institutions it challenges. A possible arrangement would support independent meeting space, interpretation, childcare and circulation of arguments, with allocation criteria that opponents can themselves question. These are proposals for the present reconstruction. They do not follow from labeling a group a counterpublic, and their effect would depend on whether participants actually control the relevant means.

Fraser distinguishes publics that form opinion from those that also make decisions, then raises the accountability of decision-making bodies to people affected outside them [@fraser1992PublicSphere, pp. 134–136]. That distinction exposes a weakness in a record that simply names a consultation forum. A resident may be fully heard while every consequential power remains elsewhere. The record must identify what can change after an intervention: the agenda, the evidence considered, an allocation, an enforcement condition or the rule of representation. Different bodies can carry different powers, but the connection between argument and decision must be stated. Independence from officials and influence over officials are separate institutional achievements. [Fraser, pp. 134–136, scan PDF pp. 15–16](https://www.unm.edu/~sromano/english540/Fraser%2C%20Subaltern%20Counterpublics.pdf#page=15).

This also prevents counterpublics from becoming another compulsory national taxonomy. People may organize around an injury that crosses languages and histories, or around an exclusion inside a recognized community. They need not prove a distinct collective essence to secure a hearing. The federation's obligation would concern access and power for the specified dispute, not the certification of an additional permanent identity. A shared world remains open when its participants can create new sites of argument without obtaining prior approval for their account of who they are.

### E. Constitutional pluralism must reach the procedures of recognition

Tully places the governing procedure within the dispute. His dialogical approach allows challenges to norms of recognition and to the procedures through which those norms are negotiated; reasonable disagreement need not disappear after a settlement [@tully2006Recognition, pp. 24–27]. This supplies a more demanding interpretation of revision than an amendment field. A person can have a route to appeal while being forbidden to challenge the categories that made the decision possible. The reconstruction should distinguish correcting a mistaken classification, changing its consequences, revising the classification rule and contesting the institution's authority to impose that rule. Success at one level cannot be presumed to settle the others. [Tully, pp. 24–27, publisher PDF pp. 34–37](https://www.ubcpress.ca/asset/9520/1/9780774812399.pdf#page=34).

Non-finality does not require every decision to wait for unanimity. Tully distinguishes dialogue from decision-making and retains other institutions as checks where negotiation leaves minorities vulnerable. He also asks whether ostensibly inclusive negotiations are governed at a distance through their agendas and resource inequalities [@tully2006Recognition, pp. 29–31]. Our response must therefore specify how an act can bind provisionally while its basis remains contestable. A decision could have an effective date, a review trigger and continuing protective duties without declaring that participation ratifies the forum's entire jurisdiction. Equally, repeated negotiation should not allow those who control essential resources to postpone relief indefinitely. A timetable distributes power when some parties can survive delay and others cannot. [Tully, pp. 29–31, publisher PDF pp. 39–41](https://www.ubcpress.ca/asset/9520/1/9780774812399.pdf#page=39).

National continuity and public contestation need different guarantees. A nation may sustain language, institutions and a claim to self-government across generations; a public may organize around one controversy and dissolve when its work changes. Giving each public a constituent veto would allow organizational multiplication to reorder the federation without a defended rule. Giving only established nations agenda rights would let inherited recognition suppress emerging opposition. A possible division would protect collective self-government through specified competences while securing direct channels for people contesting their exercise. Its legitimacy would depend on the relation between the two, including who may reopen their boundaries. Neither the age of an institution nor the novelty of its challengers determines that answer.

In the hypothetical drought, this would separate three questions currently liable to collapse. Workers need drinking water and a practicable role in decisions affecting their lives. A Treaty Nation may contest the watershed body's title to allocate water. Residents may challenge an upstream association's control of storage while declining membership in it. These claims concern intersecting material relations but do not carry identical warrants. A temporary provision agreement should identify who undertakes the duty, who funds it, what participation authorizes and which jurisdictional claims remain unsettled. The preservation of a claim cannot itself supply water; the supply of water cannot silently extinguish the claim.

Remaining together consequently requires more than portable standing and toleration of disagreement. Participants need means to develop opposition, powers through which it can affect decisions and routes to contest the procedures that distribute those powers. They may continue to disagree about the federation's story, their own representative institutions and the proper source of common authority. Composition without capture names a defensible task only if these disagreements can alter the arrangement while people retain the conditions of participation. Its political value lies in exposing the points where such alteration is blocked, including by the reconstruction's own account of reconciliation.
