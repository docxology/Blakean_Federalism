# Creating systems and delivering individuals

A research note supporting the revised opening. The final manuscript assigns the quotation identities and develops the institutional argument.

## Candidate opening discussion

Los's declaration in *Jerusalem* begins with the threat of enslavement and answers it with an act of making:

> I must Create a System, or be enslav'd by another Mans\
> I will not Reason & Compare: my business is to Create

[*Jerusalem* 10:20–21, E153; @blakeJerusalem.] The speaker is building Golgonooza, an imaginative city made through labor, conflict and the transformation of suffering. His declaration poses a question for political association: how can people make a common world without submitting their powers of creation to someone else's final account of it?

The scene gives that question its difficulty. Immediately before the declaration, Los compels his Spectre to work; immediately afterward, he demands obedience and threatens greater torment. The Spectre, in turn, invokes a sacrificial law against the children Los seeks to protect. Here creation and domination occupy the same workshop. The Spectre is Los's own divided power, identified with pride and self-righteousness, so this is also a struggle over how the maker governs himself. Reading it as a simple contest between a free individual and an external oppressor would conceal the troubling means of Los's resistance [@blakeJerusalem, 8:21–40, E151; 10:17–40, E153].

Stephen Clark makes precisely the difficulty of interpretation central to his review of Harvey Birenbaum's *Between Blake and Nietzsche*. He asks whether Los's declaration emphasizes system or creation, and objects to celebrating open process through isolated doctrinal statements. His criticism connects this problem to neglected dramatic voices and historical settings. Clark even makes doubt potentially “synonymous with energetic creation.” For a Blakean account of federation, the implication is demanding: its own organizing distinctions must remain exposed to interpretations that unsettle them [@clark1995Birenbaum, pp. 69–70].

Harry White supplies a sharper disagreement about the same declaration. At *Jerusalem* 10:21, he reads Los's refusal to reason and compare as a turn toward a priori rational construction; creation does not automatically mean imaginative truth. White also disputes Birenbaum's equation of creating truth with finding it, and challenges the priority given to levels of vision in the Fryean account he discusses. He instead distinguishes experienced particulars from reified rational constructions. This reading presses against an easy celebration of Los as the architect of emancipation: a newly invented system can still mistake its own coherence for the life it claims to comprehend [@white2005Science, pp. 116–117].

The next plate supplies the necessary companion to the declaration:

> Striving with Systems to deliver Individuals from those Systems;

[*Jerusalem* 11:5, E154; @blakeJerusalem.] The labor continues through chains, furnaces and the felt pain of threatened persons. Its object is deliverance, so the value of a system cannot be settled by its creator's independence alone. Plate 12 complicates the opposition further: mathematical fixation gives falsehood a body that can be cast off, and demonstrative science turns an adversary's weapon against him. Making something definite can expose its limits as well as enforce them [@blakeJerusalem, 11:1–7, E154; 12:10–15, E155].

*Milton* brings combination and individual identity into the same argument. The Seven Angels call themselves states and combinations of individuals, distinguishing combination in freedom and brotherhood from combination through war, sacrifice and imprisonment. The relevant distinction concerns the relations that compose a multitude. It cannot be reduced to a preference for small associations over large ones. The instruction then separates states from the identities of those within them: “States Change: but Individual Identities never change nor cease:”. Imagination itself exceeds the category of state [@blakeMilton, 32:8–38, E131–132; existing Q01–Q04].

The article distinguishes STATES understood as imaginative possibilities from NATIONS understood as historical formations through which possibilities become articulate. A federation sustains relations among them. A nation may combine several such possibilities, and a possibility may recur across different national histories. Neither relation fixes the persons involved. But permanence supplies no warrant for rule: in *Jerusalem* 31 the state Satan remains accursed so that Albion can rise from it. What must remain distinguishable is the person whose deliverance matters and the condition from which deliverance is sought [@blakeJerusalem, 31:1–18, E177–178; existing Q06].

The production of a common world also has institutions, audiences and exclusions. Konstantinos Stefanis reads Blake's 1809 exhibition as an attempt to shape a public career through the selection of paintings and the accompanying *Descriptive Catalogue*. Autonomy here involves presenting work, attracting recognition and seeking commissions. Stefanis also records earlier Royal Academy acceptances against Blake's claim of exclusion. His comparison with the Reynolds retrospective shows how selection can organize public authority; a catalogue “creates a corpus of authority.” This gives the political analogy a material question: who selects the works, experiences and participants through which the collective represents itself? [@stefanis2010Reasoned, paragraphs beginning “With his 1809 exhibition” and “By the nineteenth century”; notes 10–15; concluding comparison.]

Anne Mellor makes inclusion within that representation contested. Against the inclusive fraternity and androgyny readings she attributes to Michael Ferber and Irene Tayler, she argues that the imaginative division of labor reserves formative creation for the masculine while assigning the feminine its embodiment. The objection reaches directly into Golgonooza. A multitude's appearance within a shared form does not settle who can originate, revise or refuse that form. The proposed federation therefore needs an account of participation in making, alongside an account of membership [@mellor1982, pp. 148–150].

National imagery resists an equally comfortable resolution. The Divine Voice's election of Albion and gift of the earth's nations can sound like the authorization of a privileged center. Keeping that passage beside the account of free combination prevents a pluralist reconstruction from selecting only congenial images. The question becomes how a national formation can contribute to a common world without treating other histories as materials for its own enlargement [@blakeJerusalem, 43:1–26, E191; existing Q09].

Composition without capture names the resulting political commitment. Languages, memories, solidarities and shared achievements can sustain collective life; their value does not give any formation an exhaustive claim over its participants. Even passage requires provision: after the pilgrim simile, Los's sons clothe and feed the troubling Spectres and provide houses and gardens. Departure cannot be adequately understood as a solitary change of label [@blakeJerusalem, 73:35–54, E228–229; existing Q08 and Q23]. A federation capable of sustaining several nations must keep affiliation, interpretation and authority open to distinct questions: what people share, how they understand it, and who may act with binding consequences. The institutional reconstruction begins where those questions can be answered separately and challenged together.
